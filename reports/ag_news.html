What was tested

AG News is a collection of news articles, each filed under one of four topics: World, Sports, Business, or Science/Technology. The articles are fairly long and the topics are broad, so a model usually has plenty of clues to go on. This makes it a good first check of whether a classifier works at all, but a high score here says little about harder, finer-grained tasks.

“Testing didn't curtail homers. It's time to fess up. We were among the hordes of skeptics (sheep?) who boldly proclaimed drug-testing would blow a hole in the number of runs and home runs we'd see in 2004.”
Correct label: Sports

Every classifier was given the same 1,000 articles, drawn from the test set with an equal number from each of the 4 labels. None of these articles were used to train any of the models. Each classifier returned one label per article and, in most cases, a confidence score.

The classifiers differ in how much they were told about the task before the test, and that matters as much as the model itself when comparing scores:

  • Zero-shot (24): given only the label names, never an example
  • In-domain checkpoint (3): released models whose own training already included this task's data
  • Classical supervised (7): word-count (TF-IDF) or embedding features with a simple classifier, fitted on the task's training examples
  • Fine-tuned encoder (2): pre-trained language models adapted to the task with its training examples
  • From scratch (3): neural networks trained only on the task's training examples, starting from random weights

One of them ran on a remote service rather than the test machine, so its times include the network and unknown hardware.

Main findings

The top three classifiers scored between 94% and 95%: ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised), DeBERTa-v3-base, fine-tuned (supervised) and TF-IDF + Linear SVM, uncalibrated (supervised). When the 1,000 articles are resampled (the paired bootstrap in the full report), each of them comes first often enough that their order here should not be read as a ranking.

How to read the numbers

Accuracy
The share of articles the classifier labeled correctly. 90% means 9 out of 10 were right.
Accuracy range (95% CI)
A statistical estimate (Wilson 95% confidence interval) of the uncertainty in the measured accuracy. With only 1,000 articles, the true accuracy could plausibly be higher or lower than the measured figure. Each interval describes one classifier on its own; it is not a test of whether two classifiers differ, and overlapping intervals do not mean two classifiers are equal. The full report has paired tests for that.
Average time per article (batched)
Total classification time divided by the number of articles, with each classifier processing the sample in its usual batches or parallel requests. Model loading is not included. This measures throughput: it is not how long one request takes.
Single request
The median time to classify one item sent on its own, which is what a user waiting for one answer experiences. A dash means it was not measured in this run.
Memory
The most memory the classifier used while running, including any separate model server. A dash means it was not measured, for example for an online service.

Speed versus accuracy

Average batched times range from 23 µs per article (fastText-style network, trained from scratch (supervised)) to 4.9 s (Decision 3.0 nano), a factor of about 200,000. These are throughput figures, not response times. Sent one article at a time, 11 of the 39 classifiers with both measurements took more than twice their batched average; the largest gap is TF-IDF + Complement NB (supervised), at 25 µs batched against 1.3 ms for a single request.

Five classifiers are on the speed–accuracy frontier: no other classifier is both faster (batched) and more accurate. Every classifier not listed is beaten on both counts by at least one that is.

  • fastText-style network, trained from scratch (supervised): 92% correct, 23 µs per article batched, 42 µs single request
  • TF-IDF + Complement NB (supervised): 92% correct, 25 µs per article batched, 1.3 ms single request
  • TF-IDF + LogReg (supervised): 93% correct, 121 µs per article batched, 503 µs single request
  • TF-IDF + Linear SVM, uncalibrated (supervised): 94% correct, 121 µs per article batched, 503 µs single request
  • ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised): 95% correct, 11 ms per article batched, 9.7 ms single request

All local times come from one machine (Apple M5); the order of the faster classifiers can change on other hardware.

  • Science/Technology articles labeled as Business: 1,595 times, made by 39 of the 39 classifiers
  • Business articles labeled as Science/Technology: 773 times, made by 39 of the 39 classifiers
  • Business articles labeled as World: 670 times, made by 39 of the 39 classifiers

The first mix-up also appears in reverse in this list, so Science/Technology and Business overlap rather than one label absorbing the other.

Knowing when to trust an answer

A confidence score allows a split workflow: the classifier answers the articles it is confident about and passes the rest to a person. For each classifier, the confidence cut-off for a 95% accuracy target was chosen on one set of articles and then checked on different ones, so the figures below are not tuned to the items they are measured on.

ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised) answered 96% of the articles on its own and was 96% correct on them, followed by DeBERTa-v3-base, fine-tuned (supervised) (96%) and TF-IDF + Linear SVM, calibrated (supervised) (82%).

Cut-offs were chosen on a separate calibration sample (a second test-set sample with no items in common with this one).

Before you draw conclusions

  • The test is a sample. With 1,000 articles, an accuracy near 90% is only known to within about ±2 points. That separates good classifiers from poor ones, but not classifiers a few points apart.
  • Scores describe this setup. Some classifiers learned from labeled examples of this task and others from none; some ran locally and others on a remote service. A higher score means better under these conditions, not a better model in general.
  • Results belong to this task. A classifier that does well on AG News may do worse on different text or different labels; compare with the other benchmark task.
  • Speed depends on hardware and how the classifier is called. Batched averages measure throughput, and single-request times measure response time. Both come from one machine.
