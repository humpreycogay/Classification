Confidence types: Native probability: The model's own predicted probability, used as is: no temperature or calibrator was fitted for this benchmark. Task-calibrated: A temperature or calibrator was fitted on labeled rows of this task's train split, so the confidence and calibration metrics are task-calibrated. Release temperature: Scores divided by a temperature shipped with the model release, then normalized; nothing was fitted on this task. Normalized score: Softmax or normalized scores that sum to 1 but have no calibration step; useful for ranking labels, not as calibrated probabilities. Separate score: A confidence score defined by the service; it is not the selected label's probability.

Statistical comparisons

Every app against the top point estimate (ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised)) on the same 200 articles. Δ = accuracy difference in percentage points. Score CI: Tango score interval for a difference of paired proportions (valid with few discordant articles); bootstrap CI: percentile interval from 2,000 paired resamples of the articles (seed 20260927). McNemar p is the raw exact test; Holm p adjusts it for the 38 comparisons made. An interval containing 0 or a Holm-adjusted p of at least 0.05 indicates that the corresponding procedure did not detect a difference at the 5% level. This is not evidence that the apps are equivalent; the intervals and the p-value come from different procedures and need not agree. Rank: 95% interval of each app's rank over the same resamples; P(best): share of resamples where it ranks first (ties split). Tier comes from the rank interval, which reduces, but does not remove, the effect of choosing the top app from the same data. It is descriptive, not a pairwise test.

Accuracy vs coverage

Sort predictions by confidence and keep the most confident share: the line shows the accuracy of what is kept (x = share kept). A useful confidence rises to the left. Dashed = overall accuracy; dots = thresholds 0.5 / 0.7 / 0.9 (hover for values). Same axes in every panel. Where several predictions share one confidence, the line uses their expected accuracy in random order, since no threshold can separate them.

Reading it: Higher accuracy at the same coverage (equivalently, lower risk) is better. Caution: choose thresholds on validation data, not on this test report.

Formula

AURC = (1/n) Σk=1..n error rate of the k most confident predictions; E-AURC = AURC − AURC of an ideal ordering that ranks every correct prediction first.

Selective prediction

Operating points that do not depend on how each app scales its confidence. AURC: average error rate of the accepted predictions over every coverage (lower is better); E-AURC: AURC minus that of a perfect ordering, i.e. the part caused by the confidence ranking itself (lower is better, 0 = perfect). Coverage at target: the largest share an actual threshold can auto-accept with accuracy ≥ the target. Optimistic picks the threshold on these same test articles, so it overstates what a threshold fixed in advance would achieve; Wilson LB requires the Wilson 95% lower bound to reach the target; the threshold is still selected on this sample, so the bound is not a guarantee for future or shifted data. Validate a chosen threshold on an independent calibration or validation set before deployment. Accuracy at coverage: accuracy of the most confident share; † = that share falls inside a group of equal confidences, so no threshold gives exactly it (expected accuracy in random order is shown). The 99% target is omitted: at n = 200 even a perfect accepted set cannot show 99% (run the common or full tier).

Calibration

Does the reported confidence match the observed accuracy? ECE: average gap over 10 confidence bins. MCE: largest gap, only over bins with at least 10 predictions (bins used shown; smaller bins are too noisy). Classwise ECE: the same check for every label's probability p_label (not only the chosen one), averaged over labels. All lower is better. ⚠ marks apps whose numbers do not measure probability calibration.

Reading it: Lower ECE is better. Caution: the value depends on the binning and can hide class-specific errors; compare classwise ECE as well.

Formula

ECE = Σb (nb / n) · |accuracyb − mean confidenceb| over 10 equal-width confidence bins; MCE = maxb of the same gap over bins with enough predictions.

Definitions

  • Sample and tiers: a balanced stratified sample of the ag_news test split: n // k items per label (k labels), the n mod k leftover items going one each to the first labels in alphabetical order, drawn with the run seed. Tiers: smoke = 200, common = 1,000, full = 7,600; other sizes are custom. Only results on exactly this sample (same texts, labels, order and SHA-1) are compared. Calibration data for zero-shot apps comes from the train split, never from test.
  • Validation: before a result is accepted, the runner checks columns, row count, labels, unique sample ids, probabilities (finite, in [0, 1], rows summing to 1), pred = argmax p, the app's confidence rule, that the files were written by this run, and that CSV, metadata, seed, n and sample hash agree. Failing results are shown as invalid and never ranked.
  • Accuracy: share of articles whose predicted label equals the true label. 95% CI: Wilson score interval.
  • Macro F1: unweighted mean of the per-class F1 scores; the primary metric for imbalanced tasks, where accuracy mostly reflects the frequent labels.
  • McNemar p: exact two-sided McNemar test against the top point estimate, on the articles where exactly one of the two apps is right. Holm p: the same p-values adjusted with Holm's step-down method over all models tested (family-wise error rate 5%). Rank interval: 2.5th–97.5th percentile of each model's accuracy rank over the paired bootstrap resamples (ties share the better rank); P(best): share of resamples where it ranks first. Bootstrap leading tier: models whose percentile bootstrap rank interval includes 1. Because the top point estimate is itself chosen from this data, testing only against it would make the others look further behind than they are; the rank interval reduces, but does not remove, that effect. It is an uncertainty summary, not a test that the models are equivalent or statistically tied. The report names no single winner when the tier has more than one app.
  • Non-significant results: an interval containing 0 or a Holm p of at least 0.05 means the procedure did not detect a difference at the 5% level, not that the apps are equivalent.
  • Paired difference CI: accuracy difference d = (only-this-right − only-top-right) / n with the Tango (1998) score interval, which stays valid when few articles are discordant (a Wald interval collapses to 0–0 for two apps that always agree). Bootstrap CI: 2.5th–97.5th percentile over paired resamples of the articles (every app scored on the same resample); the seed and replicate count are in report_meta.json.
  • Pareto frontier: apps not dominated on (higher accuracy, lower time per item); the same for interactive latency and for memory where measured.
  • Interactive latency: seconds to classify one article on its own (batch 1, concurrency 1), end to end, after warm-up. Throughput: items per second with the app's own batching or concurrency. The two answer different questions: one user waiting vs a queue of work.
  • Peak memory footprint: macOS physical footprint of the app process at its peak (includes GPU memory on Apple Silicon). App + server: footprint plus the model memory of the Ollama or OpenJev server the app uses.
  • Load: constructing the classifier (weights into memory for local apps; client setup for API/server apps). Warm-up: one article before timing, which absorbs model loading inside Ollama/servers, kernel compilation and the first connection. Classification: the timed run over all articles.
  • Zero-shot / supervised / virtual: zero-shot apps decide from the label names or descriptions, with no weights or label ranking fitted on task examples. Some (confidence task-calibrated) fit one softmax temperature on labeled train rows: a single positive temperature does not change the predicted label, so their accuracy stays zero-shot, but their confidence and calibration metrics are task-calibrated; supervised baselines (TF-IDF + logistic regression, linear SVM, Complement Naive Bayes; bge-m3 embeddings + class centroids or logistic regression) are trained on the task's train split; virtual baselines are generated by the runner without a model: majority class always predicts the most frequent train label, stratified random draws a label from the train class frequencies with a generator seeded by the run seed.
  • Training: embedding the train articles (embedding baselines) plus fitting the vectorizer and classifier; reported separately and never included in load, warm-up or classification time.
  • Calibrated vs uncalibrated: the linear SVM appears twice. The calibrated variant uses sigmoid (Platt) calibration fitted by 5-fold cross-validation on the train split; the uncalibrated variant reports softmax of the raw SVM margins, which ranks labels but is not a probability.
  • Per item: classification time ÷ number of items, at each app's own batch size or concurrency.
  • Confidence: the number each app reports for gating; its definition differs by app (see the Confidence table). Most apps use max p, the largest of their per-label scores. What those scores mean:
    • Native probability: The model's own predicted probability, used as is: no temperature or calibrator was fitted for this benchmark.
    • Task-calibrated: A temperature or calibrator was fitted on labeled rows of this task's train split, so the confidence and calibration metrics are task-calibrated.
    • Release temperature: Scores divided by a temperature shipped with the model release, then normalized; nothing was fitted on this task.
    • Normalized score: Softmax or normalized scores that sum to 1 but have no calibration step; useful for ranking labels, not as calibrated probabilities.
    • Separate score: A confidence score defined by the service; it is not the selected label's probability.
  • Auto-accept ≥ t: share of predictions with confidence ≥ t (coverage) and the accuracy on that share. Fixed thresholds are not comparable across apps whose confidences are scaled differently; prefer coverage at a target accuracy.
  • Ties: predictions with equal confidence are accepted or rejected together by any threshold. Curves, AURC and accuracy at a fixed coverage use the expected accuracy with ties in random order, so they never depend on row order.
  • AURC: mean over k = 1..n of the error rate among the k most confident predictions (area under the risk-coverage curve); lower is better. E-AURC: AURC minus the AURC of an ideal ordering that puts every correct prediction first; it isolates the confidence ranking from overall accuracy (0 = perfect).
  • Coverage at target accuracy: the largest share of predictions a threshold on confidence can accept while their accuracy is at least the target, checked at every achievable threshold. Optimistic: point accuracy, threshold chosen on the test sample (overstates a threshold fixed in advance). Wilson LB: the Wilson 95% lower bound must reach the target. Both thresholds and bounds are selected on this evaluation sample; they are not a guarantee for future or shifted data, so validate a chosen threshold on an independent calibration or validation set before deployment. The 99% target is shown only from n = 1,000.
  • MCE: maximum calibration gap over confidence bins with at least 10 predictions. Classwise ECE: for each label, ECE of p_label against "the true label is this label" over all predictions, averaged over labels.
  • Top-2 accuracy: share of articles whose true label is one of the two highest p_*; ties count in expectation, so one-hot outputs score as chance for the second slot.
  • AUROC: ranking quality of confidence for separating correct from wrong predictions. ECE: expected calibration error of the reported confidence, 10 equal-width bins.
  • Brier: mean squared error of the label probability vector (p_* columns) against the one-hot truth. Log loss: mean −ln p(true label), probabilities floored at 1e-6.
