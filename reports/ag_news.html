How well do text classifiers sort AG News?

We gave 39 classifiers the same 200 articles and checked how many each one labeled correctly, how fast it worked, and how much memory it needed. This page tells the story of the results in plain language.

What was tested

AG News is a collection of news articles, each filed under one of four topics: World, Sports, Business, or Science/Technology. The articles are fairly long and the topics are broad, so a model usually has plenty of clues to go on. This makes it a good first check of whether a classifier works at all, but a high score here says little about harder, finer-grained tasks.

“Cisco Flaw Leaves Router Vulnerable to Attack. Cisco Systems issued a security advisory warning that some networks using its routers may be vulnerable to denial-of-service attacks. Devices running Internetwork Operating System and enabled for the open short…”
Correct label: Science/Technology

Every classifier was given the same 200 articles, drawn from the test set with an equal number from each of the 4 labels. None of these articles were used to train any of the models. Each classifier returned one label per article and, in most cases, a score saying how sure it was.

The classifiers fall into two broad families. Some learned from labeled examples of this very task (15 of them): they were shown thousands of articles with the right answer attached before the test. The others work from the label names alone (24 of them, often called zero-shot): they are told what the labels are, but never see an example. The second kind is harder to build well, but it can be pointed at a new task without collecting training data.

The short answer

There is no single winner. The top ten classifiers scored between 94% and 96%, and with 200 test articles the gaps between them are too small to call: on a different set of articles their order could easily change. They are ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised), Laya, Laya typed-decisions (ModernBERT-large 421M), fastText-style network, trained from scratch (supervised), DeBERTa-v3-base, fine-tuned (supervised), TF-IDF + LogReg (supervised), Decider-4B v3 (Qwen3.5-4B), bge-m3 + class centroids (supervised), bge-m3 + LogReg (supervised) and BiLSTM, trained from scratch (supervised).

The best classifier that worked from the label names alone is Laya, at 94%. That is only 1 point behind the top score, so on this task training data buys surprisingly little.

For a sense of scale, a rule that ignores the text and always picks the most common label gets 25% right. Every classifier beat that by more than ten points.

How to read the numbers

Accuracy
The share of articles the classifier labeled correctly. 90% means 9 out of 10 were right.
Likely range
A test on 200 articles is a sample, not the final word. If the same classifier were tested on fresh items, its accuracy would usually land inside this range. When two classifiers' ranges overlap a lot, treat them as roughly equal.
Time per article
How long the classifier took for each article on our test machine, working at its normal pace. Smaller is faster.
Memory
The most memory the classifier used while running, including any separate model server. A dash means it was not measured, for example for an online service.

Speed versus accuracy

Accuracy is only half the story. The fastest classifier here needs 32 µs per article; the slowest needs 5.2 s, roughly 200,000 times longer. As a rule, the larger and more accurate models are also the slower ones, so picking a classifier means deciding how much speed you are willing to trade for each extra point of accuracy.

The two classifiers below are the best trades on offer: for each of them, no other classifier is both faster and more accurate. Any classifier not on this list is beaten on both counts by at least one that is.

  • fastText-style network, trained from scratch (supervised): 94% correct, 32 µs per article
  • ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised): 96% correct, 13 ms per article

Times were measured on one machine and depend on hardware; treat them as a guide to the relative speed of the classifiers rather than as exact figures.

The most common mix-ups, counted across all 39 classifiers:

  • Science/Technology articles labeled as Business: 249 times, about 6.4 per classifier
  • World articles labeled as Science/Technology: 130 times, about 3.3 per classifier
  • Business articles labeled as Science/Technology: 112 times, about 2.9 per classifier

Mistakes like these tend to happen where the labels themselves overlap, and a person might hesitate too.

Knowing when to trust an answer

Most classifiers also say how sure they are of each answer. That makes a useful working pattern possible: let the classifier handle the articles it is confident about, and pass the rest to a person. The question is how much work it can take on before mistakes creep in.

Set the bar at 95% correct. ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised) could handle 88% of the articles on its own and still meet that bar, followed by DeBERTa-v3-base, fine-tuned (supervised) (82%) and TF-IDF + Linear SVM, calibrated (supervised) (78%). Note that a classifier's raw accuracy and its ability to judge its own answers are different skills: the most accurate classifier is not always the best at knowing when it is right.

The confidence cut-offs behind these figures were chosen on the same test articles, so check them on new data before relying on them.

Before you draw conclusions

  • The test is a sample. 200 articles is enough to separate good classifiers from poor ones, but not to rank classifiers that are within a few points of each other.
  • Results belong to this task. A classifier that does well on AG News may do worse on different text or different labels; the other benchmark task can tell quite a different story.
  • Speed depends on hardware. All timings come from one machine; on other hardware the order of the faster classifiers may change.
