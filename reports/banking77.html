How well do text classifiers sort Banking77?

What was tested

Banking77 is a collection of short messages that customers sent to an online bank, each tagged with one of 77 reasons for getting in touch, such as a lost card, a pending transfer, or a question about exchange rates. The messages are brief and many of the reasons are close cousins of one another, so telling them apart is much harder than sorting news by topic.

“Does it cost money for currency exchange services?”
Correct label: Exchange charge

Every classifier was given the same 200 messages, drawn from the test set with the 77 labels spread as evenly as possible. None of these messages were used to train any of the models. Each classifier returned one label per message and, in most cases, a score saying how sure it was.

The classifiers fall into two broad families. Some learned from labeled examples of this very task (15 of them): they were shown thousands of messages with the right answer attached before the test. The others work from the label names alone (17 of them, often called zero-shot): they are told what the labels are, but never see an example. The second kind is harder to build well, but it can be pointed at a new task without collecting training data.

The short answer

There is no single winner. The top four classifiers scored between 92% and 94%, and with 200 test messages the gaps between them are too small to call: on a different set of messages their order could easily change. They are DeBERTa-v3-base, fine-tuned (supervised), ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised), TF-IDF + LogReg (supervised) and bge-m3 + LogReg (supervised).

The best classifier that worked from the label names alone is LiquidAI d1-3B (LFM2.5-VL-3B), at 90%. That is 5 points behind the top score: roughly the price of skipping training data on this task.

For a sense of scale, a rule that ignores the text and always picks the most common label gets 2% right. 31 of the 32 classifiers beat that by more than ten points.

How to read the numbers

Accuracy
The share of messages the classifier labeled correctly. 90% means 9 out of 10 were right.
Likely range
A test on 200 messages is a sample, not the final word. If the same classifier were tested on fresh items, its accuracy would usually land inside this range. When two classifiers' ranges overlap a lot, treat them as roughly equal.
Time per message
How long the classifier took for each message on our test machine, working at its normal pace. Smaller is faster.
Memory
The most memory the classifier used while running, including any separate model server. A dash means it was not measured, for example for an online service.

Speed versus accuracy

Accuracy is only half the story. The fastest classifier here needs 14 µs per message; the slowest needs 4.0 s, roughly 300,000 times longer. As a rule, the larger and more accurate models are also the slower ones, so picking a classifier means deciding how much speed you are willing to trade for each extra point of accuracy.

The six classifiers below are the best trades on offer: for each of them, no other classifier is both faster and more accurate. Any classifier not on this list is beaten on both counts by at least one that is.

  • fastText-style network, trained from scratch (supervised): 86% correct, 14 µs per message
  • TextCNN, trained from scratch (supervised): 86% correct, 35 µs per message
  • BiLSTM, trained from scratch (supervised): 88% correct, 52 µs per message
  • TF-IDF + LogReg (supervised): 92% correct, 53 µs per message
  • ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised): 93% correct, 4.0 ms per message
  • DeBERTa-v3-base, fine-tuned (supervised): 94% correct, 4.6 ms per message

Times were measured on one machine and depend on hardware; treat them as a guide to the relative speed of the classifiers rather than as exact figures.

Where classifiers go wrong

With 77 labels and 200 messages, each label appears only about 3 times in this test, too few to say reliably which labels are hardest. The mix-ups below are more telling.

The most common mix-ups, counted across all 32 classifiers:

  • Pending transfer messages labeled as Transfer timing: 36 times, about 1.1 per classifier
  • Card arrival messages labeled as Card delivery estimate: 31 times, about 1.0 per classifier
  • Balance not updated after bank transfer messages labeled as Pending transfer: 26 times, about 0.8 per classifier

Mistakes like these tend to happen where the labels themselves overlap, and a person might hesitate too.

Knowing when to trust an answer

Most classifiers also say how sure they are of each answer. That makes a useful working pattern possible: let the classifier handle the messages it is confident about, and pass the rest to a person. The question is how much work it can take on before mistakes creep in.

Set the bar at 95% correct. ModernBERT-base, fine-tuned (supervised) could handle 91% of the messages on its own and still meet that bar, followed by TF-IDF + LogReg (supervised) (87%) and DeBERTa-v3-base, fine-tuned (supervised) (86%). Note that a classifier's raw accuracy and its ability to judge its own answers are different skills: the most accurate classifier is not always the best at knowing when it is right.

The confidence cut-offs behind these figures were chosen on the same test messages, so check them on new data before relying on them.

Before you draw conclusions

  • The test is a sample. 200 messages is enough to separate good classifiers from poor ones, but not to rank classifiers that are within a few points of each other.
  • Results belong to this task. A classifier that does well on Banking77 may do worse on different text or different labels; the other benchmark task can tell quite a different story.
  • Speed depends on hardware. All timings come from one machine; on other hardware the order of the faster classifiers may change.
