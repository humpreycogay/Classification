BiLSTM, trained from scratch

A bidirectional long short-term memory network: it reads the text one word at a time, forwards and backwards, carrying a memory vector that summarises what it has read so far. It learns everything from the task's train split, with no pre-trained weights.

02

Concepts: training from scratch vs fine-tuning

This entry is trained from scratch. It is not fine-tuned. Fine-tuning starts from a model that has already been pre-trained on huge amounts of text and only teaches it the labels. Training from scratch starts from random numbers, and our train split has to teach the model everything: which words matter, which words are similar, and which label they point to.

Training a large language model from scratch is rarely worth it for classification; it only pays off with millions of examples in an unusual domain. A small network from scratch is different: it trains in minutes, it teaches how neural classifiers work, and it is a fair reference for what pre-training adds.

03

How the model works

How it works

All three from-scratch networks share the same front and back. Each word gets a learned vector of numbers (its embedding); at the start the vectors are random, and during training words that help predict the same label drift together. At the end, a linear head turns one text vector into one score per label. What differs is the encoder in the middle: how many word vectors become one text vector. The running example is a Banking77 message, "my card has not arrived yet", label card_arrival.

diagram
forwards:   my -> card -> has -> not -> arrived -> yet   -> memory: "a card, negated, arrival"
backwards:  yet -> arrived -> not -> has -> card -> my   -> memory: "arrival, negated, a card"

[final forward memory | final backward memory | max over every step]
            -> one text vector -> linear head -> card_arrival

An LSTM reads the text one word at a time and keeps a running memory vector. At each word, learned gates decide what to remember, what to forget and what to update. After "my card has not" its memory holds something like "about a card, negated"; after "arrived" it can combine the two. That is how it understands word order and relationships across a sentence.

Bidirectional. Two LSTMs run: one left to right, one right to left. Every word is then seen with both its left and its right context. This entry joins the final memory of each direction with the maximum of every memory value over all positions, and a linear layer scores the labels.

The name. Long Short-Term Memory (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) was designed to keep information over long sequences, which earlier recurrent networks forgot. BiLSTMs were the standard for text before Transformers took over around 2018.

Padding. The texts are packed by length, so padding is never read, and a text gets the same answer alone or in a batch.

Strengths

  • Understands word order and longer-range relationships, such as "I ordered a new card two weeks ago and it still hasn't come".
  • The most expressive of the three.

Weaknesses

  • Slowest: it must read word by word and cannot process all positions at once.
  • More fiddly to train, and on keyword-driven tasks such as AG News the extra power buys little.

04

From text to numbers

A network only understands numbers, so every text goes through the same steps before training and before classification:

  1. Tokenize. Lowercase the text and split it into words and punctuation: "My card hasn't arrived!" becomes my card hasn't arrived !.
  2. Build a vocabulary, from the train split only. Every word seen at least twice (once for Banking77) gets an integer id. Id 0 is padding and id 1 is "unknown", used for any word never seen in training. AG News gives roughly 60,000-70,000 words; Banking77 roughly 2,000-3,000.
  3. Turn each text into ids and cut it to a maximum length (100 tokens for AG News, 48 for Banking77).
  4. Pad. Texts in one batch are padded with id 0 to the same length. The model masks the padding, so a text gets the same answer alone or in a batch.

The benchmark's own data preparation is unchanged: AG News text is the title and description joined as {title}. {description}, and Banking77 uses the message text. The test split is never used for any of the steps above.

05

Training procedure

Training

Training is required and will run automatically on the first benchmark run for each task. Later runs load the saved model in about a second.

  1. Split the train split: a stratified 90% trains the network; the other 10% (seed 0) is the validation set. The test split is never touched.
  2. Start from random weights (seed 0).
  3. Each step: take a batch of texts, compute the logits, measure the cross-entropy loss against the true labels, backpropagate, and let AdamW update every weight. The learning rate rises for the first 10% of steps and then falls (one-cycle schedule); gradients are clipped at norm 1.
  4. After each epoch, measure validation accuracy. Keep the best epoch; stop early when it has not improved for a few epochs (patience).
  5. Calibrate: fit one temperature T on the validation set, so that softmax(logits / T) gives honest probabilities. It changes confidence, never the chosen label.
  6. Save the weights and a JSON file with the vocabulary, settings, temperature and the per-epoch history in classifiers/scratch_bilstm/models/<task>/.

Banking77 differs because its texts are short (about 12 words), its vocabulary is small, and each epoch is only about 280 steps, so it needs many more epochs and keeps rare words.

Overfitting, and how to see it

The saved history has training loss and validation loss per epoch. Training loss keeps falling; when validation loss starts rising while training loss falls, the network is memorising the training texts instead of learning the task. Early stopping keeps the epoch before that happens. Dropout and the vocabulary cut-off also help.

Never tune on data/*_test.csv. Every choice above is made on the validation part of the train split, and the test split is evaluated once, by the harness.

06

Reproducing it, step by step

  1. Set up the environment. Creates .venv, installs PyTorch and runs the setup checks on both tasks.
    shell
    ./run setup scratch_bilstm
  2. Train and evaluate. The first run per task trains the network on the train split and saves it; later runs load the saved model.
    shell
    ./run scratch_bilstm --task ag_news --n 1000
./run scratch_bilstm --task banking77 --n 1000
  3. Inspect the training. Open models/<task>/model_<digest>.json: history has training loss, validation loss and validation accuracy per epoch, and best_epoch the epoch that was kept. Validation loss rising while training loss falls is overfitting; early stopping keeps the epoch before it.
  4. Try it on one text.
    shell
    cd classifiers/scratch_bilstm
PYTHONPATH=../../shared .venv/bin/python classifier.py "Stocks rallied after the central bank decision."
  5. Experiment. Change a recipe in CONFIGS, or pass options after --: --epochs, --lr, --train-batch-size, --train-seed, --device. A changed setting changes the model's fingerprint, so a new model is trained and kept beside the old one. Choose settings on validation accuracy, never on the test split.

07

The architecture in code

The whole architecture of this entry is one PyTorch module in classifiers/scratch_bilstm/model.py. Its input is a batch of token ids (0 means padding) and its output is one raw score (logit) per label. Everything else (tokenizing, training, calibration, saving) is shared in shared/textnn.py.

python
class BiLSTM(nn.Module):
    """A reader with memory, in both directions."""

    def __init__(self, rows, n_labels, dim=128, hidden=128, layers=1, dropout=0.3):
        super().__init__()
        self.n_labels = n_labels
        self.emb = nn.Embedding(rows, dim, padding_idx=0)
        self.lstm = nn.LSTM(dim, hidden, num_layers=layers, batch_first=True, bidirectional=True)
        self.drop = nn.Dropout(dropout)
        self.out = nn.Linear(4 * hidden, n_labels)   # last fwd + last bwd + max-pool fwd + max-pool bwd

    def forward(self, x):
        lengths = (x != 0).sum(1).clamp(min=1).cpu()
        packed = pack_padded_sequence(self.drop(self.emb(x)), lengths, batch_first=True, enforce_sorted=False)
        out, (h, _) = self.lstm(packed)                         # padding is skipped, never read
        out, _ = pad_packed_sequence(out, batch_first=True, padding_value=float("-inf"))
        pooled = out.max(dim=1).values
        last = torch.cat([h[-2], h[-1]], dim=1)                 # final forward and backward states
        return self.out(self.drop(torch.cat([last, pooled], dim=1)))

The other files are in the repository: shared/textnn.py, and classifier.py, evaluate.py, checks.py and setup.sh in classifiers/scratch_bilstm/.

On AG News, word order buys little: the BiLSTM leads the bag of embeddings by about half a point, for about ten times the training time, and none of the three beats the TF-IDF baseline (the BiLSTM is in the report's bootstrap leading tier with it). On Banking77 the three land within 1.4 points of each other, 2 to 3.5 points below TF-IDF, and above every decision model in this benchmark. The remaining gap to a fine-tuned encoder (about 94-95% and 93-94%) is what pre-training buys.

A sensible order to learn them is fastText-style first (the whole loop end to end, in minutes), then the same model on Banking77 to see the small-data problem, then the CNN and the BiLSTM to see whether word order helps and by how much.

10

Design note: why not a small Transformer

No. A Transformer is not required for classification, and trained from scratch it is often not the best choice.

  • Fine-tuned Transformers such as ModernBERT and DeBERTa beat everything on this page, but they win because of pre-training on trillions of words, not because of the architecture itself.
  • A Transformer from scratch has to learn which words relate to which (attention) on top of what the words mean, so it needs more data. On 120,000 AG News articles a small one usually lands about where the CNN or BiLSTM does, around 91-92%, while being slower and harder to tune. On Banking77's 10,000 messages it would likely do worse than the simpler models.
  • AG News is mostly a keyword problem. A bag of embeddings already gets about 92.5%, and most of the remaining errors are genuinely ambiguous articles, such as a business story about a technology company.

The practical rule: for a small model from scratch, use fastText-style or a CNN. For the best accuracy, do not train a Transformer from scratch; fine-tune a pre-trained one.
